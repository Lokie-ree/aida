## 1. K-2 Mathematics Standards Architecture & Coding System

### 1.1 Understanding Louisiana K-2 Math Standard Codes

Every Louisiana K-2 mathematics standard follows a structured coding format that enables precise identification and alignment checking. The coding system varies slightly between K-8 standards and high school courses, but for K-2, the format is consistent and straightforward.

**Standard Code Format**: `[GRADE].[DOMAIN].[CLUSTER].[STANDARD#]`

**Example**: `2.NBT.A.1`
- **2** = Grade 2
- **NBT** = Number and Operations in Base Ten (domain)
- **A** = First cluster within that domain
- **1** = First standard within that cluster

**Special Case for Kindergarten**: Kindergarten standards follow the same pattern: `K.CC.A.1` would represent Kindergarten, Counting and Cardinality domain, first cluster, first standard.

### 1.2 K-2 Domain Codes and Progressions

The Louisiana K-2 Mathematics Standards are organized into distinct domains that show clear developmental progressions:

#### Kindergarten Domains:
1. **CC** - Counting and Cardinality
2. **OA** - Operations and Algebraic Thinking
3. **NBT** - Number and Operations in Base Ten
4. **MD** - Measurement and Data
5. **G** - Geometry

#### Grade 1 Domains:
1. **OA** - Operations and Algebraic Thinking
2. **NBT** - Number and Operations in Base Ten
3. **MD** - Measurement and Data
4. **G** - Geometry

**Note**: Counting and Cardinality (CC) appears only in Kindergarten, as students transition from learning to count to using counting as a foundation for operations.

#### Grade 2 Domains:
1. **OA** - Operations and Algebraic Thinking
2. **NBT** - Number and Operations in Base Ten
3. **MD** - Measurement and Data
4. **G** - Geometry

### 1.3 Grade-Level Organization and Focus

**Kindergarten Focus**: Building number sense through counting, comparing quantities, understanding cardinality, composing and decomposing numbers to 10, and beginning place value understanding with numbers 11-19.

**Grade 1 Focus**: Developing addition and subtraction strategies within 20, extending place value understanding to 100, measuring and comparing lengths, and composing two-dimensional and three-dimensional shapes.

**Grade 2 Focus**: Achieving fluency with addition and subtraction within 100, extending place value to 1000, working with standard units of measure, and analyzing shapes by examining sides and angles.

## 2. Core K-2 Mathematics Goals & Student Outcomes

### 2.1 The Louisiana K-2 Mathematics Goal

The overarching goal for Louisiana K-2 mathematics students is threefold:

1. **Understand** fundamental mathematical concepts including number, operations, measurement, and geometry
2. **Develop fluency** with basic computational skills appropriate to grade level
3. **Apply** mathematical thinking to solve problems in context

**Mathematical Proficiency Definition**: Students are considered mathematically proficient when they demonstrate conceptual understanding (comprehension of mathematical concepts, operations, and relations), procedural fluency (skill in carrying out procedures flexibly, accurately, efficiently, and appropriately), and productive disposition (habitual inclination to see mathematics as sensible, useful, and worthwhile).

### 2.2 K-2 Mathematically Proficient Student Characteristics

A mathematically proficient student in grades K-2 demonstrates eight key practices aligned with the Standards for Mathematical Practice:

1. Makes sense of problems and perseveres in solving them
2. Reasons abstractly and quantitatively
3. Constructs viable arguments and critiques the reasoning of others
4. Models with mathematics
5. Uses appropriate tools strategically
6. Attends to precision
7. Looks for and makes use of structure
8. Looks for and expresses regularity in repeated reasoning

**K-2 Developmental Considerations**: In early grades, these practices manifest through concrete experiences. Young students might use objects to model problems, describe their mathematical thinking orally before writing it, and require teacher support to develop precision in mathematical language and notation.

## 3. K-2 Standards Framework and Cluster Organization

### 3.1 Understanding Domains and Clusters

**Domains** represent large categories of mathematics that remain consistent across grade levels, showing how mathematical concepts develop over time.

**Clusters** are groups of related standards within a domain. Cluster headings represent the "big ideas" that the standards within them address, providing teachers with a quick summary of instructional focus.

### 3.2 Kindergarten Standards by Domain

#### Counting and Cardinality (K.CC)

**Cluster A: Know number names and the count sequence**
- K.CC.A.1: Count to 100 by ones and by tens
- K.CC.A.2: Count forward beginning from a given number within the known sequence
- K.CC.A.3: Write numbers from 0 to 20; represent a number of objects with a written numeral 0-20

**Cluster B: Count to tell the number of objects**
- K.CC.B.4: Understand the relationship between numbers and quantities; connect counting to cardinality
  - K.CC.B.4a: When counting objects, say number names in standard order with one-to-one correspondence
  - K.CC.B.4b: Understand that the last number said tells the number of objects counted
  - K.CC.B.4c: Understand that each successive number name refers to a quantity that is one larger
- K.CC.B.5: Count to answer "how many?" questions
  - K.CC.B.5a: Count objects up to 20 in a line, rectangular array, or circle
  - K.CC.B.5b: Count objects up to 10 in a scattered configuration
  - K.CC.B.5c: Given a number from 1-20, count out that many objects

**Cluster C: Compare numbers**
- K.CC.C.6: Identify whether the number of objects in one group is greater than, less than, or equal to another group
- K.CC.C.7: Compare two numbers between 1 and 10 presented as written numerals

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Counting and Cardinality is foundational to all mathematical work. Students must understand that the last number counted represents the total quantity (cardinality principle) and that each number in the sequence is one more than the previous (successor principle). These understandings cannot be rushed and require extensive concrete experience.

#### Operations and Algebraic Thinking (K.OA)

**Cluster A: Understand addition as putting together and adding to, and understand subtraction as taking apart and taking from**
- K.OA.A.1: Represent addition and subtraction with objects, fingers, mental images, drawings, sounds, acting out situations, verbal explanations, expressions, or equations
- K.OA.A.2: Solve addition and subtraction word problems within 10
- K.OA.A.3: Decompose numbers less than or equal to 10 into pairs in more than one way
- K.OA.A.4: For any number from 1 to 9, find the number that makes 10 when added to the given number
- K.OA.A.5: Fluently add and subtract within 5

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Kindergarteners should see addition and subtraction equations and are encouraged to write them, but writing equations is not required. The focus is on understanding the operations conceptually through multiple representations before formal symbolic notation.

#### Number and Operations in Base Ten (K.NBT)

**Cluster A: Work with numbers 11-19 to gain foundations for place value**
- K.NBT.A.1: Gain understanding of place value
  - K.NBT.A.1a: Understand that numbers 11-19 are composed of ten ones and additional ones
  - K.NBT.A.1b: Compose and decompose numbers 11-19 using place value
  - K.NBT.A.1c: Record each composition or decomposition using a drawing or equation

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: This standard lays essential groundwork for Grade 1 place value work. Students must understand that "teen" numbers are special because they represent one ten and some ones, not just a collection of individual objects.

#### Measurement and Data (K.MD)

**Cluster A: Describe and compare measurable attributes**
- K.MD.A.1: Describe measurable attributes of objects such as length or weight
- K.MD.A.2: Directly compare two objects with a measurable attribute in common

**Cluster B: Classify objects and count the number of objects in each category**
- K.MD.B.3: Classify objects into given categories; count numbers in each category and sort categories by count

**Cluster C: Work with money**
- K.MD.C.4: Recognize pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters by name and value

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Kindergarten measurement focuses on direct comparison without formal measurement tools. Students compare by placing objects side by side (length) or holding them (weight), developing the language and concepts that will support later standard measurement.

#### Geometry (K.G)

**Cluster A: Identify and describe shapes**
- K.G.A.1: Describe objects in the environment using names of shapes and relative positions
- K.G.A.2: Correctly name shapes regardless of orientation or size
- K.G.A.3: Identify shapes as two-dimensional or three-dimensional

**Cluster B: Analyze, compare, create, and compose shapes**
- K.G.B.4: Analyze and compare shapes using informal language
- K.G.B.5: Model shapes in the world by building shapes from components
- K.G.B.6: Compose simple shapes to form larger shapes

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Kindergarten geometry emphasizes recognizing and describing shapes in various orientations. Students must learn that a square rotated 45 degrees is still a square, and that shapes have defining attributes (number of sides, angles) versus non-defining attributes (color, size, orientation).

### 3.3 Grade 1 Standards by Domain

#### Operations and Algebraic Thinking (1.OA)

**Cluster A: Represent and solve problems involving addition and subtraction**
- 1.OA.A.1: Use addition and subtraction within 20 to solve word problems with unknowns in all positions
- 1.OA.A.2: Solve word problems with three addends whose sum is less than or equal to 20

**Cluster B: Understand and apply properties of operations and the relationship between addition and subtraction**
- 1.OA.B.3: Apply properties of operations to add and subtract
- 1.OA.B.4: Understand subtraction as an unknown-addend problem

**Cluster C: Add and subtract within 20**
- 1.OA.C.5: Relate counting to addition and subtraction
- 1.OA.C.6: Add and subtract within 20, demonstrating fluency for addition and subtraction within 10

**Cluster D: Work with addition and subtraction equations**
- 1.OA.D.7: Understand the meaning of the equal sign; determine if equations are true or false
- 1.OA.D.8: Determine the unknown whole number in an addition or subtraction equation relating three whole numbers

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: First grade is where students transition from primarily concrete representation to beginning symbolic fluency. The emphasis on understanding the equal sign as "the same as" rather than "the answer is" prevents persistent misconceptions. Students must experience equations like 8 = 3 + 5 (not just 3 + 5 = 8) to develop relational thinking.

#### Number and Operations in Base Ten (1.NBT)

**Cluster A: Extend the counting sequence**
- 1.NBT.A.1: Count to 120 starting at any number less than 120; read and write numerals and represent numbers with written numerals

**Cluster B: Understand place value**
- 1.NBT.B.2: Understand that the two digits of a two-digit number represent amounts of tens and ones
  - 1.NBT.B.2a: 10 can be thought of as a bundle of ten ones—called a "ten"
  - 1.NBT.B.2b: Numbers from 11-19 are composed of a ten and additional ones
  - 1.NBT.B.2c: Numbers 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 refer to one, two, three... tens (and 0 ones)
- 1.NBT.B.3: Compare two two-digit numbers based on meanings of tens and ones digits using >, =, < symbols

**Cluster C: Use place value understanding and properties of operations to add and subtract**
- 1.NBT.C.4: Add within 100 including adding a two-digit and one-digit number and adding a two-digit number and a multiple of 10
- 1.NBT.C.5: Given a two-digit number, mentally find 10 more or 10 less without counting
- 1.NBT.C.6: Subtract multiples of 10 from multiples of 10 in the range 10-90

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: First grade place value work extends Kindergarten's foundation with teen numbers to all two-digit numbers. The understanding that 34 means 3 tens and 4 ones (not just 3 and 4) is foundational for all future work with multi-digit arithmetic. Students need extensive work with base-ten blocks and other manipulatives to internalize this concept.

#### Measurement and Data (1.MD)

**Cluster A: Measure lengths indirectly and by iterating length units**
- 1.MD.A.1: Order three objects by length; compare lengths of two objects indirectly using a third object
- 1.MD.A.2: Express the length of an object as a whole number of length units by laying multiple copies of a shorter object end to end

**Cluster B: Tell and write time**
- 1.MD.B.3: Tell and write time in hours and half-hours using analog and digital clocks

**Cluster C: Represent and interpret data**
- 1.MD.C.4: Organize, represent, and interpret data with up to three categories

**Cluster D: Work with money**
- 1.MD.D.5: Determine the value of a collection of coins up to 50 cents (single coin type only, not mixed)

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: First grade introduces the concept of iterating (repeating) a unit to measure length, but does not yet require standard units. Students might measure with paper clips or blocks, learning that measurement requires units of equal size with no gaps or overlaps. This builds the foundation for standard measurement in Grade 2.

#### Geometry (1.G)

**Cluster A: Reason with shapes and their attributes**
- 1.G.A.1: Distinguish between defining attributes versus non-defining attributes
- 1.G.A.2: Compose two-dimensional and three-dimensional shapes to create a composite shape
- 1.G.A.3: Partition circles and rectangles into two and four equal shares; describe using halves, fourths, quarters

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: First grade geometry deepens understanding of shape attributes. Students must articulate why a triangle is a triangle (three straight sides that connect) regardless of its color, size, or orientation. Early fraction work through equal shares begins here, setting the stage for more formal fraction work in Grade 3.

### 3.4 Grade 2 Standards by Domain

#### Operations and Algebraic Thinking (2.OA)

**Cluster A: Represent and solve problems involving addition and subtraction**
- 2.OA.A.1: Use addition and subtraction within 100 to solve one- and two-step word problems with unknowns in all positions

**Cluster B: Add and subtract within 20**
- 2.OA.B.2: Fluently add and subtract within 20 using mental strategies; know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers by end of Grade 2

**Cluster C: Work with equal groups of objects to gain foundations for multiplication**
- 2.OA.C.3: Determine whether a group of objects (up to 20) has an odd or even number of members
- 2.OA.C.4: Use addition to find the total number of objects arranged in rectangular arrays with up to 5 rows and 5 columns

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Grade 2 is pivotal for achieving computational fluency within 20. The standard requiring students to "know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers" means automatic recall, not counting. This fluency is essential for success with multi-digit computation. The introduction of arrays as equal groups lays groundwork for multiplication in Grade 3.

#### Number and Operations in Base Ten (2.NBT)

**Cluster A: Understand place value**
- 2.NBT.A.1: Understand that the three digits of a three-digit number represent amounts of hundreds, tens, and ones
- 2.NBT.A.2: Count within 1000; skip-count by 5s, 10s, and 100s
- 2.NBT.A.3: Read and write numbers to 1000 using base-ten numerals, number names, and expanded form
- 2.NBT.A.4: Compare two three-digit numbers based on meanings of hundreds, tens, and ones digits using >, =, < symbols

**Cluster B: Use place value understanding and properties of operations to add and subtract**
- 2.NBT.B.5: Fluently add and subtract within 100 using strategies based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction
- 2.NBT.B.6: Add up to four two-digit numbers using strategies based on place value and properties of operations
- 2.NBT.B.7: Add and subtract within 1000 using concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value
- 2.NBT.B.8: Mentally add 10 or 100 to a given number 100-900, and mentally subtract 10 or 100
- 2.NBT.B.9: Explain why addition and subtraction strategies work, using place value and properties of operations

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Grade 2 extends place value understanding to 1000, but the expectation is different for operations. Students must be fluent (efficient, accurate, flexible) with addition and subtraction within 100, but work within 1000 is exploratory, using models and drawings. The requirement to explain strategies (2.NBT.B.9) is critical for developing mathematical reasoning.

#### Measurement and Data (2.MD)

**Cluster A: Measure and estimate lengths in standard units**
- 2.MD.A.1: Measure the length of an object by selecting and using appropriate tools
- 2.MD.A.2: Measure the length of an object twice using length units of different lengths; describe how measurements relate to unit size
- 2.MD.A.3: Estimate lengths using units of inches, feet, centimeters, and meters
- 2.MD.A.4: Measure to determine how much longer one object is than another

**Cluster B: Relate addition and subtraction to length**
- 2.MD.B.5: Use addition and subtraction within 100 to solve word problems involving lengths given in the same units
- 2.MD.B.6: Represent whole numbers as lengths from 0 on a number line diagram

**Cluster C: Work with time and money**
- 2.MD.C.7: Tell and write time from analog and digital clocks to the nearest five minutes using a.m. and p.m.
- 2.MD.C.8: Solve word problems involving dollar bills, quarters, dimes, nickels, and pennies

**Cluster D: Represent and interpret data**
- 2.MD.D.9: Generate measurement data by measuring lengths of several objects to the nearest whole unit; show measurements by making a line plot
- 2.MD.D.10: Draw a picture graph and a bar graph (with single-unit scale) to represent data with up to four categories

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Grade 2 introduces standard measurement units, a major conceptual leap from Grade 1's use of non-standard units. Students must understand that centimeters are smaller than inches, so more centimeters are needed to measure the same length. The number line work (2.MD.B.6) connects measurement to number, preparing students for fraction number lines in Grade 3.

#### Geometry (2.G)

**Cluster A: Reason with shapes and their attributes**
- 2.G.A.1: Recognize and draw shapes having specified attributes; identify triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons, and cubes
- 2.G.A.2: Partition a rectangle into rows and columns of same-size squares and count to find the total number
- 2.G.A.3: Partition circles and rectangles into two, three, or four equal shares; describe shares using halves, thirds, fourths; recognize that equal shares need not have the same shape

**Critical Pedagogical Note**: Partitioning a rectangle into rows and columns of squares (2.G.A.2) is foundational for area understanding in Grade 3. The recognition that equal shares don't need to have the same shape (2.G.A.3) is a sophisticated understanding that prevents misconceptions about fractions and equivalence.

## 4. K-2 Vertical Progression: The Power of Developmental Coherence

### 4.1 Why K-2 Vertical Progression Matters for RAG

Vertical progression in K-2 mathematics represents the most powerful aspect of the Louisiana standards for AI-assisted instruction. Unlike upper grades where concepts become increasingly abstract, K-2 shows how foundational concepts emerge from concrete experience and develop into increasingly sophisticated mathematical thinking. This progression enables the platform to:

1. **Anticipate developmental readiness**: Identify whether students have the prerequisite concrete experiences
2. **Suggest appropriate scaffolding**: Recommend manipulatives, representations, or language supports based on where students are in the progression
3. **Differentiate effectively**: Adjust content to match developmental level while maintaining cognitive demand
4. **Build coherent units**: Design instruction that explicitly bridges prior and future learning

### 4.2 Sample Vertical Progression: Understanding and Using Addition

**Kindergarten: K.OA.A.1 - Represent addition with objects, fingers, mental images, drawings, sounds, acting out situations, verbal explanations, expressions, or equations**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Multiple concrete representations of joining situations
- **Cognitive Demand**: Modeling real-world situations with physical objects
- **Key Understanding**: Addition means putting together or adding to
- **Typical Student Work**: Uses cubes or fingers to show 3 + 2; may count all objects starting from 1

**Kindergarten: K.OA.A.5 - Fluently add and subtract within 5**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Developing automaticity with the smallest numbers
- **Cognitive Demand**: Immediate recall or very quick mental strategies
- **Key Understanding**: Some addition facts can be known without counting
- **Typical Student Work**: Quickly states that 2 + 3 = 5 without counting

**Grade 1: 1.OA.C.6 - Add and subtract within 20, demonstrating fluency for addition and subtraction within 10. Use mental strategies such as counting on, making ten, decomposing numbers leading to ten, using the relationship between addition and subtraction, and creating equivalent but easier sums**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Transitioning from counting all to counting on; developing derived fact strategies
- **Cognitive Demand**: Strategic thinking about number relationships
- **Key Understanding**: Numbers can be decomposed and recomposed to make computation easier; addition and subtraction are related operations
- **Typical Student Work**: For 8 + 5, student might think "8 + 2 is 10, and 3 more is 13" (decomposing 5 into 2 + 3 to make a ten first)

**Grade 1: 1.OA.D.7 - Understand the meaning of the equal sign, and determine if equations involving addition and subtraction are true or false**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Developing relational thinking rather than operational thinking about equals
- **Cognitive Demand**: Evaluating relationships rather than computing
- **Key Understanding**: The equal sign means "the same as," not "the answer is"
- **Typical Student Work**: Recognizes that 7 = 3 + 4 is true without needing to see it written as 3 + 4 = 7; understands that 5 + 2 = 4 + 3 is true because both sides equal 7

**Grade 2: 2.OA.B.2 - Fluently add and subtract within 20 using mental strategies. By end of Grade 2, know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Achieving automaticity with basic facts through understanding, not drill
- **Cognitive Demand**: Immediate recall of facts within 20
- **Key Understanding**: All addition facts within 20 can be retrieved from memory; subtraction facts are related to known addition facts
- **Typical Student Work**: Immediately states that 8 + 7 = 15 and 15 - 7 = 8; no visible strategy or counting needed

**Grade 2: 2.OA.A.1 - Use addition and subtraction within 100 to solve one- and two-step word problems involving situations of adding to, taking from, putting together, taking apart, and comparing, with unknowns in all positions**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Applying fluent computation to solve complex word problems
- **Cognitive Demand**: Problem comprehension, representation, multi-step planning, and computation
- **Key Understanding**: Addition and subtraction can be used to solve a wide variety of problem types; problems can be represented with equations before solving
- **Typical Student Work**: For "Maya had 43 stickers. She gave 15 to her brother and then bought 27 more. How many does she have now?", student writes 43 - 15 + 27 = ? or solves in steps, explaining the reasoning for each operation

### 4.3 Critical Pedagogical Shifts in K-2 Addition and Subtraction Progression

**Shift 1: From Count All to Count On to Derived Facts**
- Kindergarten students typically "count all" (count the first set, count the second set, count all objects together)
- Grade 1 students learn to "count on" from the larger number (for 3 + 8, start at 8 and count "9, 10, 11")
- Late Grade 1 and Grade 2 students use derived facts (knowing 8 + 8 = 16, so 8 + 7 = 15)
- Platform must recognize that simply teaching procedures without this conceptual progression creates procedural students who cannot think flexibly about numbers

**Shift 2: From Objects to Symbols**
- Kindergarten: Primary work is with physical objects; equations are introduced but not required
- Grade 1: Frequent movement between objects/drawings and equations; equations become more prominent
- Grade 2: Equations are primary representation; objects/drawings are used for complex problems or when explaining reasoning
- Platform must not rush students past concrete representations before they are developmentally ready

**Shift 3: From Single-Step to Multi-Step**
- Kindergarten: Single-step situations only (took away some, how many left?)
- Grade 1: Mostly single-step but may combine operations in context of three addends
- Grade 2: Explicitly multi-step problems requiring planning and sequencing
- Platform must help teachers distinguish between computational difficulty and problem-solving complexity

**Shift 4: From Teacher-Provided Strategies to Student-Generated Strategies**
- Kindergarten: Teacher shows multiple strategies; students choose and try them
- Grade 1: Students begin to develop their own strategies based on number relationships they notice
- Grade 2: Students select strategies based on numbers involved; explain why strategies work
- Platform must support teachers in facilitating strategy development rather than prescribing single methods

### 4.4 Sample Vertical Progression: Place Value Understanding

**Kindergarten: K.NBT.A.1 - Gain understanding of place value**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Recognizing that teen numbers are composed of a ten and some ones
- **Cognitive Demand**: Seeing a group of ten as a unit (early unitizing)
- **Key Understanding**: Numbers 11-19 are special because they contain one ten
- **Typical Student Work**: Uses ten-frames to show that 14 is a full ten-frame plus 4 more; may record as "14 = 10 + 4"
- **Critical Foundation**: This is students' first encounter with the idea that "1" can represent one ten, not just one object

**Grade 1: 1.NBT.B.2 - Understand that the two digits of a two-digit number represent amounts of tens and ones**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Extending teen number understanding to all two-digit numbers; understanding decade numbers
- **Cognitive Demand**: Abstracting place value from specific quantities to general principle
- **Key Understanding**: Any two-digit number can be decomposed into tens and ones; the position of a digit determines its value
- **Typical Student Work**: Represents 34 with 3 ten-sticks and 4 ones cubes; writes "34 = 30 + 4" or "34 = 3 tens and 4 ones"

**Grade 1: 1.NBT.B.3 - Compare two two-digit numbers based on meanings of the tens and ones digits, using >, =, and < symbols**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Using place value understanding to compare quantities without counting
- **Cognitive Demand**: Reasoning about relative magnitude using place value
- **Key Understanding**: When comparing two-digit numbers, tens are compared first; if tens are equal, ones are compared
- **Typical Student Work**: Explains that 47 > 39 because 4 tens is more than 3 tens, without needing to count to 47 and 39

**Grade 1: 1.NBT.C.4 - Add within 100, including adding a two-digit number and a one-digit number, and adding a two-digit number and a multiple of 10**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Using place value understanding to make addition strategies more efficient
- **Cognitive Demand**: Decomposing numbers by place value to add
- **Key Understanding**: Tens and ones can be added separately; sometimes ones must be composed into a new ten
- **Typical Student Work**: For 34 + 7, student might add 4 + 7 = 11, recognize this is 1 ten and 1 one, and combine with the 3 tens to get 41. Or for 34 + 20, student quickly states 54 by combining the tens

**Grade 2: 2.NBT.A.1 - Understand that the three digits of a three-digit number represent amounts of hundreds, tens, and ones**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Extending place value understanding to three digits; understanding that 100 is ten tens
- **Cognitive Demand**: Generalizing place value pattern to larger numbers
- **Key Understanding**: Place value pattern extends infinitely; each place is ten times the value of the place to its right
- **Typical Student Work**: Represents 347 with 3 hundred-flats, 4 ten-sticks, and 7 ones cubes; writes "347 = 300 + 40 + 7"

**Grade 2: 2.NBT.B.5 - Fluently add and subtract within 100 using strategies based on place value**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Achieving fluency with two-digit computation using place value strategies
- **Cognitive Demand**: Selecting and applying efficient strategies based on the numbers involved
- **Key Understanding**: Multiple strategies exist; efficiency depends on the numbers and context
- **Typical Student Work**: For 58 + 37, might add tens (50 + 30 = 80), add ones (8 + 7 = 15), combine (80 + 15 = 95). For 62 - 28, might add up from 28 to 30 (that's 2), then 30 to 62 (that's 32 more), so 2 + 32 = 34

**Grade 2: 2.NBT.B.7 - Add and subtract within 1000 using concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value**
- **Pedagogical Focus**: Extending place value strategies to three-digit numbers with support from models
- **Cognitive Demand**: Managing more complex computations while maintaining place value reasoning
- **Key Understanding**: Same place value principles apply to larger numbers; models help organize thinking
- **Typical Student Work**: For 347 + 256, uses base-ten blocks or drawings, adding hundreds (300 + 200 = 500), tens (40 + 50 = 90), and ones (7 + 6 = 13), then combines (500 + 90 + 13 = 603)

### 4.5 Critical Pedagogical Shifts in K-2 Place Value Progression

**Shift 1: From Ones to Tens to Hundreds**
- Kindergarten: Introduction to ten as a unit (teen numbers only)
- Grade 1: Full understanding of two-digit numbers as tens and ones
- Grade 2: Extension to three-digit numbers with hundreds
- Platform must ensure students have fully internalized each level before moving to the next

**Shift 2: From Concrete to Abstract**
- All grades in K-2 require concrete materials, but the sophistication increases
- Kindergarten: Ten-frames and loose objects
- Grade 1: Unifix cubes, base-ten blocks (rods and units), bundling materials
- Grade 2: Continued use of base-ten blocks but increasing work with drawings and mental strategies
- Platform must not skip manipulative use, even in Grade 2

**Shift 3: From Counting to Reasoning**
- Kindergarten: May count to verify place value representations
- Grade 1: Begins to reason about place value without counting
- Grade 2: Fluently reasons about place value relationships
- Platform must help teachers recognize when students are still relying on counting and need more concrete experience

## 5. Mathematical Practices in K-2 Context

### 5.1 Standards for Mathematical Practice: K-2 Manifestations

The eight Standards for Mathematical Practice apply to all grade levels, but manifest differently in K-2 than in upper grades. The platform must support teachers in recognizing and developing these practices with developmentally appropriate expectations.

**MP1: Make sense of problems and persevere in solving them**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students use concrete objects to model problems; they try multiple approaches when first attempts don't work; they check answers by modeling or re-solving in a different way
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Help creating low-floor, high-ceiling tasks; language for encouraging persistence; questions that prompt students to make sense rather than follow procedures
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Young students give up quickly or look immediately to teacher for answer; teachers rescue too quickly rather than allowing productive struggle

**MP2: Reason abstractly and quantitatively**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students contextualize problems (tell stories about numbers); decontextualize (represent with equations or drawings); attend to meaning of quantities (this 5 represents 5 apples, not abstract 5)
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Questions that prompt students to tell stories about numbers; prompts to represent problems multiple ways; emphasis on units and what numbers mean in context
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Students compute without attending to what numbers represent; teachers accept answers without requiring students to explain if they make sense in context

**MP3: Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students explain their thinking using objects and drawings; they say whether they agree or disagree with classmates and explain why; they ask clarifying questions of peers
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Sentence frames for mathematical argument; norms for respectful critique; tasks that generate multiple strategies to discuss
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Students say "I agree" without justification; teacher does all the reasoning and students just listen; students only share when they have the right answer

**MP4: Model with mathematics**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students use objects, drawings, and diagrams to represent problem situations; they use equations to model relationships; they check if their model matches the problem
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Exposure to multiple modeling tools (ten-frames, number lines, bar models, etc.); time to model before solving; prompts to check if model matches situation
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Students jump to computation without modeling; teachers provide models rather than having students construct them; students can't explain what their model represents

**MP5: Use appropriate tools strategically**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students choose from available tools (cubes, base-ten blocks, number lines, hundred charts); they can explain why they selected a particular tool; they recognize when a tool is not helping
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Well-organized tool access; instruction in how tools work; opportunities to discuss tool selection
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Students grab random tools without thinking; teachers tell students which tool to use; tools are decorative rather than functional

**MP6: Attend to precision**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students use accurate mathematical vocabulary (e.g., "equals" not "makes"); they line up digits carefully; they include units in measurements; they state full equations (8 = 3 + 5, not just "8")
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Modeling precise language; explicit instruction in mathematical vocabulary; emphasis on full sentences and explanations
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Teachers accept imprecise language; students confuse similar terms (corner vs. vertex, side vs. edge); computation lacks organization

**MP7: Look for and make use of structure**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students recognize patterns (teen numbers all have ten; doubles are easy facts); they use known relationships (if I know 5 + 5 = 10, then 5 + 6 = 11); they notice the structure of the number system
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Questions that highlight structure; tasks that make structure visible; naming of structures when students notice them
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Students see each problem as brand new; teachers tell structures rather than helping students discover them; structures remain implicit

**MP8: Look for and express regularity in repeated reasoning**
- **K-2 Manifestation**: Students notice what stays the same when problems change (when adding zero, the number doesn't change); they generalize from repeated experiences (adding makes the number bigger); they can predict based on patterns
- **Teacher Support Needed**: Multiple problems that vary systematically; questions about what's always true; encouragement to make generalizations
- **Common K-2 Challenge**: Teachers present isolated problems rather than sequences; students don't notice patterns across problems; generalizations remain unstated

### 5.2 Connecting Practices to K-2 Content Standards

The platform must help teachers see where mathematical practices naturally connect to content standards. Examples:

**When teaching K.OA.A.3 (decompose numbers to 10):**
- MP7 (structure): Students notice that 5 can be decomposed many ways but the total stays 5
- MP8 (repeated reasoning): Students generalize that every number has multiple decompositions
- Platform suggestion: "Have students find all the ways to make 7. Ask: What do you notice? What stays the same? What changes? Can you predict how many ways there are to make 8?"

**When teaching 1.NBT.B.3 (compare two-digit numbers):**
- MP2 (reasoning quantitatively): Students must attend to place value meaning of digits, not just their face value
- MP6 (precision): Students must use >, =, < correctly and read them accurately
- Platform suggestion: "Show students pairs like 54 and 47. Ask: Which is greater? How do you know? What do you look at first? When do you need to look at ones?"

**When teaching 2.OA.A.1 (solve two-step word problems):**
- MP1 (make sense): Students must identify what's happening in the problem before computing
- MP4 (model): Students need to represent each step with drawings or equations
- Platform suggestion: "Present problem without numbers first: 'Maya had some stickers. She gave some to her brother. Then she bought some more. Now how many does she have?' Discuss what operations are needed before adding numbers."

## 7. Critical Pedagogical Principles for K-2 RAG Implementation

### 7.1 Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) Sequence

Louisiana K-2 mathematics instruction must follow the research-based CRA instructional sequence:

**Concrete**: Students use physical manipulatives to model mathematical concepts
- **Kindergarten**: Heavy emphasis on concrete; abstract work is minimal
- **Grade 1**: Begins with concrete, transitions to representational, introduces abstract
- **Grade 2**: Still uses concrete for new concepts and complex problems, increasing representational and abstract work

**Representational**: Students use drawings or diagrams to represent concepts
- **Kindergarten**: Very simple drawings (circles for objects); teacher often does the drawing
- **Grade 1**: Students draw representations independently; may use structured drawings (ten-frames, simple diagrams)
- **Grade 2**: More sophisticated drawings; introduces number lines, bar models for comparison

**Abstract**: Students use numbers and symbols without concrete or representational support
- **Kindergarten**: Minimal; may write simple equations (3 + 2 = 5) but not required
- **Grade 1**: Increasingly expected but not exclusive; most work still includes concrete or representational
- **Grade 2**: Abstract is common but not universal; concrete still used for new concepts

**RAG Application**: Platform must never skip phases of CRA sequence. When generating lessons, it should:
- Include concrete materials for new concepts regardless of grade
- Transition to representational only after concrete mastery
- Include abstract work but maintain connections to concrete and representational
- Flag when teacher-generated content jumps to abstract too quickly

### 7.2 Number Sense Development Before Computational Fluency

Louisiana K-2 standards emphasize that number sense must develop before and alongside computational procedures:

**Number Sense Components in K-2**:
- **Cardinality**: Understanding that the last number counted tells "how many"
- **One-to-one correspondence**: Matching one count word to one object
- **Magnitude understanding**: Knowing that 8 is more than 5 without counting
- **Part-whole relationships**: Seeing that 5 can be 3 and 2, or 4 and 1, or 5 and 0
- **Place value understanding**: Recognizing that 34 means 3 tens and 4 ones, not 3 and 4
- **Flexible thinking about numbers**: Knowing multiple ways to make or decompose a quantity

**RAG Application**: Platform should prioritize number sense activities over rote computational practice:
- For Kindergarten, extensive work with quantities, comparing, and decomposing before formal addition
- For Grade 1, deep understanding of teen numbers and two-digit place value before multi-digit addition
- For Grade 2, multiple strategies and mental math before standard algorithms
- Flag when content emphasizes procedures over understanding

### 7.3 Problem Types and Unknown Positions

Louisiana K-2 standards explicitly require students to solve problems with unknowns in all positions, not just "result unknown" problems. The platform must understand problem type structures from Glossary Table 1:

**Addition and Subtraction Problem Types**:
- **Add To**: Start unknown, change unknown, or result unknown
- **Take From**: Start unknown, change unknown, or result unknown  
- **Put Together/Take Apart**: Both addends unknown, one addend unknown, or total unknown
- **Compare**: Difference unknown, bigger unknown, or smaller unknown

**RAG Application**: Platform should:
- Generate or suggest problems representing all problem types and unknown positions
- Recognize that "change unknown" and "start unknown" are more challenging than "result unknown"
- Suggest appropriate scaffolding for more complex problem types
- Flag when teacher materials only include result unknown problems

### 7.4 Mathematical Language Precision in K-2

While K-2 students are still developing language skills, mathematical language precision matters:

**Critical Language Distinctions**:
- "Equals" means "the same as," not "the answer is"
- "More" and "less" vs. "greater than" and "less than" (comparative vs. formal language)
- "Ten" (the number 10) vs. "a ten" (a unit of ten ones)
- "Add" vs. "plus" (operation vs. symbol)
- "Corner" vs. "vertex" and "side" vs. "edge" (2D vs. 3D terminology)

**RAG Application**: Platform should:
- Model precise language in all generated content
- Suggest sentence frames that use precise terminology
- Flag imprecise language in teacher-generated content
- Distinguish between informal language (acceptable for student exploration) and formal language (teacher should model)

### 7.5 Standards as Floor, Not Ceiling - K-2 Context

Louisiana standards represent minimum expectations, but K-2 application differs from upper grades:

**Below the Standard**: When students are not yet ready
- **Acceptable**: Extensive concrete work before moving to representational
- **Acceptable**: Working with smaller numbers within the standard's range (e.g., addition within 10 before addition within 20 for Grade 1)
- **Acceptable**: More scaffolding and teacher support
- **Not Acceptable**: Completely different content (e.g., teaching counting when standard requires addition)
- **Not Acceptable**: Watering down cognitive demand (e.g., students always given answer format rather than constructing their own)

**Above the Standard**: When students are ready for extension
- **Acceptable**: Larger numbers within reason (e.g., adding within 30 instead of 20 for advanced Grade 1 students)
- **Acceptable**: More complex problem contexts requiring multistep reasoning
- **Acceptable**: Earlier introduction of representations (e.g., number lines in Grade 1)
- **Not Acceptable**: Skipping conceptual development to rush to procedures
- **Not Acceptable**: Teaching algorithms before understanding (even for advanced students)

## 10. K-2 Mathematics Vocabulary and Concept Glossary

### 10.1 Essential K-2 Mathematics Terms

This glossary provides K-2-specific definitions for terms that appear in the standards. The platform should use these definitions when generating content and checking alignment.

**Addition and subtraction within 5, 10, or 20**: Addition or subtraction of two whole numbers with whole number answers, and with sum or minuend in the specified range. Example: 8 + 2 = 10 is addition within 10; 14 - 5 = 9 is subtraction within 20.

**Cardinality**: The understanding that the last number counted tells how many objects are in a set. This is a critical K-2 concept that must develop before computational fluency.

**Compose**: To put together (often refers to numbers). Example: Composing ten ones into one ten; composing 5 as 3 + 2.

**Counting on**: A strategy for addition where the student starts with one addend and counts up from there. Example: For 5 + 3, student says "5... 6, 7, 8" rather than counting "1, 2, 3, 4, 5" then "6, 7, 8."

**Decompose**: To break apart (often refers to numbers). Example: Decomposing 7 as 4 + 3, or decomposing 34 as 30 + 4, or decomposing one ten into ten ones.

**Expanded form**: Writing a multi-digit number as a sum of place values. Example: 34 = 30 + 4, or 347 = 300 + 40 + 7.

**Fluently**: Efficiently (in a reasonable amount of time), accurately, and flexibly (using multiple strategies as appropriate). Fluency requires understanding, not just memorization.

**Making ten**: A strategy for addition where one addend is decomposed to create a sum of ten with part of the other addend. Example: For 8 + 5, student might think "8 + 2 is 10, and 3 more is 13" (decomposing 5 into 2 + 3 to make the ten first).

**One-to-one correspondence**: The understanding that when counting, each object is counted once and only once, and each object gets one and only one count word.

**Place value**: The value of a digit based on its position in a number. Example: In 34, the 3 means 30 (or 3 tens) and the 4 means 4 (or 4 ones).

**Properties of operations**: Mathematical rules that are always true. In K-2, students use commutative property of addition (3 + 5 = 5 + 3), associative property of addition (2 + 3 + 4 can be grouped as 2 + (3 + 4) or (2 + 3) + 4), and identity property of addition (5 + 0 = 5). Students don't need to know the formal names, but they should understand and use these properties.

**Subitizing**: Instantly recognizing how many objects are in a small set without counting. Example: Seeing •• •• and immediately knowing there are 4 dots. This skill is foundational for number sense.

**Ten-frame**: A visual tool with two rows of five boxes, used to develop number sense and understanding of quantities to 10 and relationships to 10.

**Unitizing**: Understanding that a group can be counted as one unit. Example: Seeing ten objects as "one ten" rather than only as "ten ones." This is foundational for place value understanding.
